        Abstract. Choosing how many executors a Spark SQL query receives
        trades response time against reserved capacity. Fixed pools over-reserve
        or delay queries, dynamic allocation grows only after backlog appears,
        and profiling-based tuners pay for trial runs that ad-hoc queries can-
        not amortise. PRISM sizes each query from physical-plan features be-
        fore execution, biases that choice against under-provisioning with cross-
        validated safety margins, calibrates requests to the available executor
        supply, and permits one grow-only correction after the first completed
        shuffle. On the 22 official TPC-H queries, three latency predictors meet
        every query-mean target with 11.1–13.0% fewer core-hours than a four-
        executor reference, and the training-selected predictor meets 90.9%. On
        a separate eight-query panel, two predictors meet every idle target, but
        under CPU contention no PRISM variant exceeds 37.5% compliance,
        and with concurrent tenants none attains the best makespan, slowdown
        or reservation. Safety margins add 8.7 percentage points of compliance,
        whereas runtime correction and an idle capacity cap reduce it. Plan-
        based sizing helps on unloaded workloads; under contention, calibration
        and late executor growth limit it.

        Keywords: Spark SQL · Predictive resource allocation · Runtime adap-
        tation · Resource calibration · Concurrent execution



1     Introduction

Spark SQL [1] queries differ widely in the parallelism they can use. The executor
count assigned to a query determines both how quickly it finishes and how much
capacity it withholds from other work: a pool sized for the heaviest aggregation
over-reserves for a selective scan, while a smaller pool delays the queries that
need parallelism.
   A static pool imposes one compromise on every query. Spark’s Dynamic
Resource Allocation (DRA) [3] grows the pool only after task backlog appears,
which can limit the benefit for short queries. Adaptive query execution (AQE) [2]
2       A V Kiran et al.

changes how a query executes, not how much capacity it receives. Profiling-
based tuners such as Ernest [10] and CherryPick [11] decide earlier but rely on
trial executions, a cost recurring jobs can amortise and ad-hoc queries cannot.
AutoExecutor [20] avoids trial runs by predicting Spark SQL runtime across
executor counts.
    Prediction before execution raises three questions: (i) how to handle asym-
metric error, since under-provisioning can violate a latency target whereas mod-
est over-provisioning only reserves more capacity; (ii) whether mid-query evi-
dence can correct an underestimate, given that new executors must still register;
and (iii) how to reconcile predictions with the executor supply on a contended or
shared host. To our knowledge, these questions have not been evaluated jointly
for per-query executor sizing.
    Predictive Resource Inference for Spark Management (PRISM) answers each
question with one mechanism. A plan-based predictor chooses an initial executor
count from Catalyst plan features, and safety margins selected by grouped cross-
validation bias that choice against under-provisioning (i). A shuffle-triggered cor-
rection re-estimates demand once, after the first completed shuffle-map stage,
and may request growth but never shrinkage (ii). Supply-aware calibration clamps
every request to executors that can be granted (iii). The two stages are com-
plementary: the first acts early but without runtime evidence, the second with
evidence but late in the query.
    PRISM builds on established techniques (Catalyst statistics, standard learn-
ers, grouped cross-validation and AutoExecutor-style runtime prediction [1,20]);
its contribution is combining them with asymmetric margins, one runtime cor-
rection and supply calibration in one controller, evaluated end to end. We ask
whether advance prediction improves the latency–resource trade-off on the of-
ficial workload at idle (RQ1); on a fixed panel with unseen query families and
against profiling-based tuners (RQ2); under CPU contention (RQ3); under ten-
ant sharing (RQ4); and which components drive compliance, at what overhead
(RQ5). Our contributions are the controller (Sects. 3–4); an evaluation with
same-load reference targets, charged profiling probes, and separate slowdown
and makespan metrics (Sect. 5); and evidence that, on a single host, plan-based
sizing meets every query-mean target at lower allocated cost when unloaded but
not under contention, with margins helping and late correction and an idle cap
hurting compliance (Sects. 6–7).

2   Related Work
Adaptive Query Execution and Query Optimisation. AQE revises physical plans
from runtime statistics [2], and runtime and learned optimisers improve plan
choices [17,18,19]. They decide how a query executes; PRISM decides how much
capacity it receives and runs with AQE enabled. The two are complementary,
although their interaction is not isolated here.

Reactive Resource Management. DRA dynamically creates and terminates ex-
ecutors in response to pending tasks and idle executors [3]. It does not require
           PRISM: Two-Stage Predictive Resource Allocation for Spark SQL          3

any model, but it will not act until backlog exists; PRISM determines the num-
ber of executors before the query starts and may grow it once, after the first
completed shuffle. DRA is one of our baselines.

Predictive Resource Allocation. AutoExecutor is the closest previous study; it
predicts Spark SQL run time as a function of executor count and uses these pre-
dictions to determine suitable parallelism for each query [20]. PRISM-L follows
the same predictive principle. However, PRISM also predicts the number of ex-
ecutors directly (PRISM-C), applies safety margins selected by cross-validation,
adds one shuffle-triggered correction, and calibrates executor requests under
CPU load and tenant sharing. AutoExecutor is not evaluated in this paper, so we
cannot conclude whether PRISM is better than AutoExecutor. Fine-grained re-
source models [15] and unified analytics optimisers [16] aim for similar objectives
but operate over larger configuration spaces.

Profiling-Based Configuration Tuning. Ernest builds statistical models based on
sampled executions [10], CherryPick uses Bayesian optimisation [11], LOCAT
tunes Spark SQL online, taking data size into account [12], and database tuners
search large configuration spaces [13,14]. Their search cost can be amortised
across recurring workloads but must be paid per query for ad-hoc work, whereas
PRISM reuses its frozen predictors, with executor cores and memory fixed. Our
Lyu- and RIOS-inspired rules and profiling baselines are repository adaptations
of the cited ideas, not reimplementations.

Shared-Cluster Scheduling and Interference. Cluster managers and sharing poli-
cies [4,5,6] and interference-aware schedulers [7,8,9] allocate resources across ap-
plications. PRISM sizes each query individually and neither performs admission
control nor enforces fairness across tenants.

    SLA compliance is the share of queries whose mean latency over repetitions
meets Tq , so 100% does not require every repetition to pass. J and L̄ weight
queries equally, whereas the core-hour ratio r divides total core-hours by those
of Static-4 and so weights long queries more. The metrics are not interchangeable:
latency and compliance describe query outcomes, r measures reserved capacity,
and only W reflects unused reservation.


4     PRISM Design

In Fig. 1, offline training fits predictors f1 , f2 and selects margins m1 , m2 , which
are then frozen. Per query, prediction yields a requested count n̂s for stage s ∈
{1, 2}, calibration makes it a grantable count ns , and runtime correction decides
whether to grow from n1 to n2 .


4.1   Stage 1: Plan-Based Prediction

The plan vector x contains input bytes, join depth, estimated shuffle bytes,
directed acyclic graph (DAG) depth and fan-out. PRISM-C predicts the label
n∗q of Eq. (1) directly; PRISM-L predicts log latency over counts and selects
from the predicted curve. Each uses six learners: random forest (RF), XGBoost
(XGB), LightGBM (LGBM), Ridge regression, support vector regression (SVR)
and a multilayer perceptron (MLP). Every PRISM policy enables both stages.


4.2   Safety Margins

We apply safety margins because an underestimate can exceed Tq , whereas an
overestimate only increases R. The margin ms for each stage is selected jointly
with the model settings by grouped five-fold cross-validation (CV). PRISM-C
          PRISM: Two-Stage Predictive Resource Allocation for Spark SQL         5

applies this margin prior to rounding. PRISM-L selects the smallest count whose
estimated latency falls within a factor of 1.15 e−ms of its estimated minimum
latency. Increasing ms therefore reduces the acceptable difference between esti-
mated latencies and favours larger counts.


4.3   Supply-Aware Calibration

Our calibration function C clamps each request to the available executor supply
S. Under CPU load, we use the cap max(1, min(n̂, ⌊0.9A⌋)), where A is a CPU-
derived slot estimate. During idle execution, all four workers are permitted, and
under concurrent execution, requests are capped at the number of free slots.


4.4   Stage 2: Shuffle-Triggered Correction

When the initial executors have registered, the query begins. A monitor then
polls Spark’s REST API every 250 ms. Each response provides statistics z:
completed-stage shuffle bytes and the active-stage task count, with the con-
figured number of partitions as a fallback. Once the first completed shuffle-map
stage has been processed (only once), f2 returns n̂2 , and the calibrated counts
give the grow-only update

                 nnext = max(n1 , n2 ),     1 ≤ n1 , n2 ≤ S ≤ 4.              (3)

Growth is requested asynchronously only when n2 > n1 . Afterwards, there is
no further resizing or shrinking, and added executors help only if they register
before the remaining work completes. All comparisons involving frozen models
disable feedback.


5     Experimental Methodology

Platform. Spark 3.5.1 and OpenJDK 17 run on an Intel Core i5-12450H with
12 hardware threads and about 15 GB of memory. Four standalone workers host
two-core, 1536 MB executors; the driver has 2 GB, and AQE is enabled. Con-
tention is induced by K ∈ {0, 4, 8} CPU-spinning processes, which hold no ex-
ecutor slots.

Workloads. All queries use TPC-H at scale factor 10 in Parquet. Training uses
480 queries in 14 families and testing 150 queries, which enter neither fitting
nor margin selection; preliminary observations preceded training expansion. CV
over fixed-count initial decisions selects C-MLP for deployment. The official
benchmark (RQ1) runs the 22 official queries with 18 policies and three timed
repetitions at K = 0. The broader idle workload (RQ1) uses 97 completed query
identities with identical repetition counts for every policy. The live panel (RQ2,
RQ3) has eight hash-selected queries, two from each of four groups (official, two
variant sets, unseen families), with 23 measured policies and one timed delivery

6.1    RQ1: Margins Enable Full Compliance at Lower Cost, Post Hoc
On all 22 official queries (Table 1), L-RF, L-LGBM and L-Ridge meet every
query-mean target while saving 12.9%, 13.0% and 11.1% of allocated core-hours
relative to Static-4 (unrounded totals). Individual repetitions can still miss (L-
RF twice, L-Ridge once, L-LGBM never), and L-Ridge has the lowest PRISM

the training-selected C-MLP meets 90.9% of targets at r = 0.90, against 63.6%

four executors throughout, like Static-4, so its saving reflects shorter measured
runtime rather than allocation and indicates the scale of run-to-run variation

6.2   RQ2: Idle Gains Hold on the Live Panel, with Headroom
      Unused
The live panel, separate from the official benchmark, fixes query composition
across regimes and includes unseen families. At idle (Table 2), C-RF and L-Ridge
meet every target at r = 0.98 and 0.99; C-RF is the lowest-cost full-compliance
predictor; C-MLP meets seven of eight targets at r = 0.97 and J = 2.59; and
L-LGBM has the lowest predictor mean latency, 12.67 s, but misses one target.
    Fully compliant predictors have r near one, while the hindsight oracle reaches
full compliance at r = 0.81, so most of the opportunity remains unrealised.
Rankings also shift: L-RF meets every official target but 62.5% here, while C-
RF moves from 90.9% to 100%. With one delivery per query, one miss moves
compliance by 12.5 percentage points.
    With profiling charged, Ernest and CherryPick use 2.94 and 4.36 times the
reference core-hours, an immediate per-query cost that reuse could amortise for
recurring workloads. For C-MLP, an exact paired signed-rank latency test gives
a Holm-adjusted p = 1.000 over all 34 original idle comparisons, so the observed
latency differences are not statistically resolved.
Answer to RQ2. Partially: two predictors comply fully, but with little saving.

7                         Discussion

Selecting a policy and a metric. On the repeated benchmark, a 90% requirement
favours C-SVR as the lowest-cost qualifying PRISM policy, and full compliance
favours L-LGBM by cost and L-Ridge by latency; on the live idle panel, C-RF
and L-Ridge meet every target and L-LGBM misses one. Deployment needs a
           PRISM: Two-Stage Predictive Resource Allocation for Spark SQL            11

declared requirement and workload-specific calibration, and only the training-
selected policy is a deployable choice. With unit weights Static-4 has J = 3
(three unit ratios, no violation), and C-MLP’s lower live score accepts one missed
target, a preference the weight study shows can reverse; strict compliance should
be checked before minimising J or core-hours.

Limitations and threats to validity. Single host: one machine and at most four ex-
ecutors per query bound both generality and possible savings. Limited repetition:
live panels use one delivery per query, so variance is not estimated. Variability:
identically allocated policies (L-Ridge, Static-4) differ in latency, so small dif-
ferences in r and L̄ may be noise. Profiling cost: idle profiling is charged per
query but tenant studies reuse profiles, and PRISM’s offline labelling cost is not
charged. Core-hours: reserved capacity, not utilisation, energy or billing. Sharing
scope: admission control, fairness and arrival patterns are untested. Generalis-
ability: results cover TPC-H at one scale, fixed executor sizes and CPU-only
interference, and equal coverage does not control order effects.


8   Conclusion

Advance prediction improves the latency–resource trade-off on the unloaded of-
ficial workload: three latency policies meet every query-mean target with 11.1–
13.0% fewer allocated core-hours. It does not under CPU contention or tenant
sharing, and the training-selected configuration falls short of full compliance.
Safety margins improve compliance; late growth and capacity caps reduce it. An
advantage over AutoExecutor, multi-node behaviour and statistically resolved
latency differences remain unestablished. Next steps are availability-based cali-
bration, measured registration delay, repeated trials on larger clusters, and mem-
ory and storage interference.
